# [Modulation Identification] Automated Modulation Recognition of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles Based on Ultra-Light Convolutional Neural Networks [with code attached]

Introduction Adaptive Modulation Classification (AMC) is a key technology for analyzing signal characteristics, with wide applications in both military and civilian fields, especially in the field of UAVs [1] and internet [2] and [3]. In this domain, AMC applications include communication security and interference management, signal quality optimization, and spectrum perception and management.
For the communication security and interference management of UAV wireless systems, AMC plays a crucial role. It identifies and categorizes signals from various sources, which is a critical process for detecting interference sources and protecting UAV systems from malicious signals. In terms of signal quality optimization, AMC is particularly beneficial in dynamic and complex environments that occur during UAV communication. It helps these systems automatically choose the best modulation method under current conditions, improving transmission quality and efficiency. Moreover, AMC is essential for spectrum perception and management in crowded and frequently used frequency bands. It enables UAVs to analyze spectrum usage and make strategic channel selection decisions, thereby enhancing spectrum utilization efficiency.
In recent years, deep learning (DL) has made significant advancements in signal classification applications, including AMC [6], [7], [8], specific transmitter recognition [9], [10], [11], and interference recognition [12], [13]. The introduction of DL into AMC, especially through convolutional neural networks (CNN) [7], recurrent neural networks (RNN) [14], and graph neural networks (GNN) [15], has promoted the automatic extraction of salient features from large datasets, significantly improving performance.
O 'Shea and West [16] first publicly released the AMC dataset, namely RML2016.10a, and proposed a VT-CNN 2 model for AMC. This was the first validation of the effectiveness of dynamic alliances for asset management companies. Subsequently, the goal of DL-based AMC methods is to design models with higher classification performance, faster inference speed, smaller model size, and fewer labeled samples [17], [18], [19], [20], [21].
To enhance classification performance, Huynh-The et al. [22] proposed a hybrid structure called MCLDNN that combines one-dimensional convolution, two-dimensional convolution, and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) to extract and fuse spatiotemporal features from the same phase (I), orthogonal (Q), and IQ components. It outperforms previous deep learning-based AMC methods. Similarly, Huynh-The et al. [23] introduced MCNet based on multiple convolutional blocks with residual connections and asymmetric convolutional kernels, achieving outstanding performance.
Although these methods exhibit better performance, they introduce significant increases in both the number of parameters and computational requirements. As a result, many researchers have proposed various lightweight AMC methods [24], [25], especially aimed at reducing computation load and parameter count. However, compared to advanced structures like MCLDNN, these lightweight and low-complexity AMC models often demonstrate significant performance shortcomings.
Recognizing the potential for further reduction in computational demand and parameter size, we developed a new lightweight and low-complexity architecture specifically designed for AMC in environments with limited storage and computing resources. This model not only matches the classification performance of MCLDNN with an average accuracy of 62.47% in RML 2016.10a but also competes in computational speed with Separable CNN (SCNN) and MCNet. Notably, it processes interference on typical edge devices within less than 1 ms and is close to 0.01 ms on GPUs. Most notably, our model is significantly smaller than existing DL models for AMC, having only 9751 parameters.
AMC method based on ULCNN is illustrated in Figure 1. The proposed AMC method consists of three main components: 1) IQ channel fusion module based on CV convolution; 2) feature mining and dimensionality reduction (FMDR) module based on separable convolution; and 3) cross-layer feature fusion (CLFF) module. Initially, the IQCF module utilizes CV convolution to explore the correlation between IQ channels. Subsequently, six cascading FMDR modules are employed. These serve dual purposes of extracting deep features and reducing feature dimensions. The process culminates with the CLFF module, which integrates features from different levels; these integrated features are then input into the final fully connected (FC) layer for modulation classification.

Furthermore, two strategies were adopted to enhance performance: 1) data augmentation (DA) and 2) adaptive learning rate (ALR). Data augmentation was applied during preprocessing to increase the number of training samples, while ALR was utilized during training to dynamically adjust the learning rate to ensure it aligns with the training progress. The detailed procedure of the proposed ULCNN-based AMC is listed in algorithm 1.

1) IQCF module: The IQCF module employs CV convolution to fuse the coherent and orthogonal components of signal samples and find their correlation [39].
2) FMDR module: The primary goal of the FMDR module is to extract deep features while progressively reducing feature dimensions. This is achieved through a structured FMDR module that integrates separable convolution, channel mixing (CS), and channel attention (CA) mechanisms.
3) CLFF module: The CLFF module facilitates the fusion of these deep features from the fourth, fifth, and sixth FDMR modules through addition operations.
In this section, the loss function is the cross-entropy loss function, which is a common loss function for multi-class classification problems. Adam and ALR are used as optimizers. The rule of ALR is that if the validation loss cannot decrease within ten epochs, the learning rate will be decayed to 80% of its original value.
The simulation experiment in this paper uses a simulated environment including NVIDIA GTX 1080Ti, Python 3.7, Tensorflow 1.14 and Keras 2.1.4. The results come from experiments using the RML 2016.10a dataset. The dataset is divided into three different subsets: 1) 77,000 samples for training; 2) 33,000 samples for validation; 3) 110,000 samples for testing.
The parameters NCV and NPW are configured as 16 and 32 respectively, while Ske is fixed at 5. For the learning process, an initial learning rate of 0.001 is adopted, along with 200 periods and a batch size of 128. Additionally, we compared and analyzed the performance of ULCNN with several well-known methods: MCLDNN [22] and MCNet [23], which are considered strong AMC methods, and PET-GCDNN [24] and SCNN [25], which are typical lightweight AMC methods.

The classification performance of the proposed ULCNN and its comparison with other methods are shown in Figure 3. Without DDA, ULCNN performs excellently, with an average accuracy of 57.13% across all SNRs, surpassing MCLDNN and PET-GCDNN by 0.91% and 0.39%, respectively. Moreover, ULCNN outperforms MCNet in classification accuracy and significantly leads SCNN.
As the introduction of DA, all five methods have seen significant performance enhancements, with improvements ranging from 1.94% to 6.33%, highlighting the importance of training sample size. Although MCLDNN achieved an impressive average accuracy of 62.55%, ULCNN still holds competitive performance, with an average classification accuracy only lagging by 0.08%. Notably, under higher SNR (≥ 6dB), both MCLDNN and ULCNN achieved accuracies exceeding 92%, significantly surpassing the approximately 84% and 85% accuracy achieved without DA.

As shown in Figure 5(a), achieving optimal classification performance when SKe = 5. Although the ULCNN with smaller SKe = 3 kernels is more efficient in terms of computation and parameter usage, its classification performance is about 1% lower than that of the similar product with SKe = 5. Conversely, compared to the SKe = 5 model, the ULCNN with SKe = 7 kernels not only performs poorly in classification accuracy but also leads to higher computational and parameter costs. This demonstrates that selecting SKe = 5 in our study is reasonable, balancing the trade-off between classification efficacy and resource consumption.
Figure 5(B) illustrates the impact of neuron count on classification performance. The dimensionality of the output feature vector MCLFF is shaped by the neuron count, both too high or too low dimensions are unfavorable for classification accuracy. Moreover, a higher number of neurons translates into increased computational and parameter requirements.
The role of the number of FMDR modules in classification performance is explored in Figure 5(c). For this analysis, the CLFF module is omitted. Classification accuracy averages slightly above 62% when NFDMR = 4 through 6, but there is a significant drop when NFDMR = 2 or 3.
The reason for integrating the functions of the fourth to sixth FMDR modules lies in their comparable classification performance under different SNR levels. For example, the characteristics of the sixth FMDR module perform well in low SNR environments (SNR ≤ -6dB), while the characteristics of the fifth module perform superiorly in higher SNR environments (SNR > -6dB).
Conclusion: This paper proposes an AMC method based on ULCNN, which combines various techniques to design a lightweight and low-complexity DL model. Simulation results show that the proposed AMC method has better performance advantages and is very suitable for edge scenarios with limited computing power and storage space. In future work, our goal is to design hardware-friendly DL models, accelerate their interference through methods such as neural network pruning, neural network quantization, and KD.
